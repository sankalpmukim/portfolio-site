# How Many Times Do They Say "Here We Go" in The Big Bang Theory?

If you have watched even a handful of *The Big Bang Theory* episodes, you already know the sound. Someone is about to sit in the wrong spot, correct a holiday, or launch a lecture nobody asked for. A roommate inhales, and then:

> **Here we go.**

It is the show's audible eye-roll. I wanted a real count, so I went through the full series — **279 episodes, 72,160 lines, 711,242 words** — and pulled every time a character actually says it.

**The headline: 56 times, in 49 episodes.** That is roughly once every 5.7 episodes. Not a running gag in every scene. A ritual that shows up just often enough to feel like part of the furniture.

![Words spoken, "here we go" by speaker, and "here we go" by season](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/overview.png)

The left chart is how much each person talks. The middle chart is who says the phrase. They are not the same ranking, and that is the whole story.

## The first one is still the best one

The series does not ease into this. The **pilot** is the first hit.

Penny sits in Sheldon's spot. Sheldon asks what the difference is. Penny asks the same question back. Leonard, who has heard this speech before we have, mutters *here we go* — and Sheldon unloads **76 words** about radiators, cross-breezes, and parallax distortion.

That is the template. The phrase is not the joke. The phrase is the warning label on the joke.

The last one is in **S12E14**. Sheldon himself says it, then immediately starts ranting about Leonard's unpermitted deck. The catchphrase bookends the series: Leonard warning us about Sheldon, then Sheldon warning us about Sheldon.

Only **two** of the 56 are "here we go *again*" — Sheldon in S04E15, Penny in S08E24. Everyone else just says the three-word version, as if once is enough.

## Howard says it the most. Sheldon does not.

This was the first result that surprised me.

![Who says "here we go" across all 12 seasons](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/hwg_by_speaker.png)

| Speaker | Count | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Howard | 15 | 27% |
| Leonard | 12 | 21% |
| Sheldon | 9 | 16% |
| Penny | 7 | 13% |
| Raj | 6 | 11% |
| Amy | 2 | 4% |
| Bernadette | 2 | 4% |
| Driver / Mark Hamill / Tattooist | 1 each | 5% |

Sheldon talks more than anyone in the show. He still is not the person who says "here we go." Howard is, and it is not close.

That is not because Howard has more lines. He is fourth in total dialogue. Relative to how much he actually speaks, he is even more of an outlier:

![How often each character says "here we go" versus how much they talk](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/hwg_rate_vs_talk.png)

- **Howard:** 2.03 times per 1,000 lines
- **Leonard:** 1.00
- **Raj:** 0.92
- **Penny:** 0.74
- **Sheldon:** 0.60
- **Bernadette:** 0.49
- **Amy:** 0.39

Howard is saying it **more than three times as often as Sheldon**, once you adjust for screen time. Leonard is the roommate who has to live with the lectures. Howard is the friend who narrates them, DMs the D&D campaigns, and starts half the group activities. Both versions of the phrase belong to people standing *next to* the chaos, not inside it.

Guest speakers get one each: a tattooist (S03E03), a taxi driver (S04E21), and **Mark Hamill officiating a wedding** (S11E24). Even Luke Skywalker does not escape the bit.

## Season 8 is a binge. Season 5 is a drought.

The phrase does not grow or fade in a clean arc. It pulses.

![Season-by-season count of "here we go"](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/hwg_by_season.png)

| S1 | S2 | S3 | S4 | S5 | S6 | S7 | S8 | S9 | S10 | S11 | S12 |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 6 | 6 | 3 | 5 | **1** | 6 | 4 | **9** | 3 | 3 | 6 | 4 |

Season 5 has a single instance, in the paintball opener, and it is Sheldon using the phrase as a mission briefing rather than a warning. Every other season has at least three.

Season 8 is the peak, with nine. By then the group is larger, the plots are busier, and the phrase has more jobs: starting a brainstorming session, walking into a temple, cheering Leonard up, groaning at a wedding-date lecture. It is no longer only the "Sheldon is about to talk" button.

Plot it as a timeline and the pattern is even clearer. Early seasons cluster around Leonard. Later seasons spread across Howard, Penny, and the rest of the group. The red stars are the ones where Sheldon speaks next.

![Every "here we go" in the series, with red stars when Sheldon speaks next](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/hwg_timeline.png)

A few episodes stack more than one:

- **S06E23** has **three**, all during the D&D game, with Howard as dungeon master: *"Okay, here we go. You find yourselves…"*
- S01E11, S02E16, S02E20, S07E21, and S08E05 have two each.

When the phrase clusters, it is usually because someone is running a scene — a campaign, a photoshoot, an invention brainstorm — not because the writers forgot they already used it.

## The Sheldon effect

The most interesting number is not who says it. It is **who talks next**.

Sheldon speaks next in **16 of 56 cases (29%)**, more than anyone else. Raj is second, with 11, but Raj is often the excited person *causing* the "here we go," not the rant that follows it.

![Whether Sheldon speaks next after each character's "here we go"](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/hwg_sheldon_next_split.png)

Leonard is the smoking gun. When Leonard says it, Sheldon follows **half the time** (6 of 12). That is the roommate frequency: Leonard hears the inhale, names the storm, and then Sheldon arrives on schedule.

Howard's 15 are more mixed. Four are followed by Sheldon. Five are followed by Raj. A lot of Howard's uses are "the activity is starting now," not "brace yourselves." Penny is in between: 2 of 7. Raj, Amy, and Bernadette never get Sheldon as the next speaker.

The heatmap makes the two jobs of the phrase obvious.

![Who says "here we go" versus who speaks next](/articles/here-we-go-big-bang-theory/hwg_speaker_x_next.png)

The hottest cell in the whole grid is **Leonard → Sheldon**. The second hottest is **Howard → Raj**. Same three words, two different sitcom machines: the warning, and the kickoff.

Mark Hamill's single "here we go," while officiating, is followed by Sheldon. Of course it is.

## After the warning, Sheldon talks the longest

When someone else says the phrase and Sheldon takes the next line, he does not just take it. He keeps it.

On average, the next speaker's consecutive-word run is:

- **Sheldon: 24.4 words**
- Penny: 24.3
- Raj: 14.9
- Leonard: 14.4
- Howard: 9.7

Penny's average is inflated by one long fantasy monologue in S08E14. Sheldon's average is the default setting. He simply talks longer than everyone else in the series — **27.6% of all words on 20.8% of the lines** — which is exactly what "here we go" is bracing for.

Leonard triggers **five of Sheldon's seven longest post-"here we go" monologues**. If you hear Leonard say it, sit down. You are about to get a lecture on furniture, silverware, pathogens, or Isaac Newton.

## So what is the phrase actually doing?

After 56 instances, it is not one gag. It is three.

**1. The warning.** Leonard (and sometimes Penny or Howard) hears Sheldon wind up. The line is for the audience as much as the room: *you already know what comes next, and so do they.* That is why the pilot instance is perfect, and why it keeps working eleven seasons later.

**2. The kickoff.** Howard as dungeon master, Raj starting a movie, Penny taking Leonard shopping, Sheldon launching paintball. Same words, opposite energy. The group is about to *do* something, not endure something.

**3. The self-own.** When Sheldon says it, he is usually starting his own bit — proton decay, Episode I, an unpermitted deck. He does not hear it as a complaint. He hears it as a drumroll.

That split is why Howard can lead the leaderboard without being the punchline. He lives in jobs 1 and 2. Sheldon is the reason job 1 exists, which is why he barely needs to say the words himself.

The rarity helps. Fifty-six times in 279 episodes is not spam. It is a small, reliable tell. Once you notice it, you cannot stop hearing it — which, if you have ever lived with a Sheldon, is the point.
